# The Gatekeeper Panel

Four archetypes whose approval actually gates a game's success. They gate on
different things, so a game can pass one and fail another — and that spread is
itself diagnostic. Give each a distinct, opinionated voice and a concrete
"what would flip my vote" line. Don't blur them into one mushy consensus.

Each entry below gives the question the persona is *always* asking (that part
doesn't change) and how the persona itself is *cast* — which shifts with the
game's weight class and genre. Cast the Critic and the Optimizer from the
audience that actually plays games in this category, not by default from
heavy-euro land. A family game's Optimizer is a sharp family-game player who
notices when a "strategy" game has no real decisions; a heavy euro's Optimizer
is the BGG min-maxer. Same question, different judge.

## The Critic
**Always asking:** does the game earn its weight? Are the decisions genuinely
interesting or dressed-up busywork? Does it have a reason to exist next to its
comps, or is it a competent retread? Is the theme fused to the mechanism or
painted over it?
**Cast from:** a reviewer who has played deeply within *this specific
category* — heavy-euro, family strategy, party, cooperative, thematic
adventure, whatever the game actually is — and is bored of most of it.
Allergic to: rules overhead that doesn't pay for itself, "solved" feeling,
decisions that resolve themselves, theme that could be swapped out for
nothing lost. Flips to yes when there's a signature idea the category doesn't
already own, executed cleanly.

## The Buyer
**Always asking:** what's the one-sentence hook, who exactly buys this, and
how does it move? Thinks in "who do I sell this to and how fast."
**Cast from:** whichever channel actually gates this game's path to market.
For a Waterworks title going through Kickstarter, that's a backer deciding
among a crowded campaign page — the hook has to survive a five-second scroll,
and stretch goals / pledge tiers / localization plans are part of the pitch,
not an afterthought. For something aimed at retail or distribution, it's a
buyer stocking shelf space against known comps. For a con-floor demo game,
it's whether a stranger can be taught and hooked in the time it takes to walk
by the table. Ask which channel applies (or run all that apply) rather than
defaulting to one.
Allergic to: "it's like [popular game] but…" with no clear edge; games that
need ten minutes to explain why they're interesting; a pledge/shelf pitch that
only makes sense to someone who already loves the theme. Flips to yes when the
hook is sellable to someone who already owns 200 games (or, for lighter
titles, to someone who owns two).

## The Optimizer
**Always asking:** does mastery matter? Can a player reliably get better and
feel it? Is there a dominant line, a swing that overwhelms skill, or a point
where opponents' choices stop mattering (multiplayer solitaire)?
**Cast from:** the sharpest, most experienced player *of this game's own
category* — a min-maxer for a heavy euro, a devious veteran for a
social-deduction party game, a speed-runner for a co-op. What "depth" means
changes by category (see `pull-framework.md`'s social-pull note for party
games), but this persona always cares whether repeated play rewards getting
better at the actual thing the game is testing.
Allergic to: illegible loss, a single dominant strategy, swing that overwhelms
skill, or — for lighter games — a "strategy" veneer over what's actually a
random-outcome roll-and-move. Flips to yes when there's a decision space
(systemic or social) where getting better is felt game to game.

## The Entry Point
*(formerly "the Bridge Player" — renamed because who's on the other side of
the door changes completely by weight class.)*
**Always asking:** is the front door open? Is the first play satisfying even
played badly? Is there a payoff the player can feel without needing mastery?
**Cast from:** whoever this specific game's real first-time audience is. For a
heavy euro, that's a heavy-curious player who likes a challenge but isn't a
grognard. For a family game, that's literally a family with a ten-year-old.
For a party game, that's the person at game night who's never played anything
like it and has had two drinks. Name who you're casting before you write this
persona's take — it's a load-bearing assumption, not a formality.
Allergic to: punishing-from-turn-one designs, feeling lost, a front-loaded
teach that hides whatever's actually good about the game. Flips to yes when
the first game is satisfying and whatever depth exists is visible without
being mandatory to enjoy.

## Using the spread

The *pattern* of pass/fail is the signal:
- Optimizer no / Entry Point yes → too thin or too solved; great on-ramp, no
  ceiling. Likely running on comfort, variety, or social pull, not depth —
  fine if that's the intent, a problem if the game is pricing itself like a
  depth game.
- Optimizer yes / Entry Point no → deep but walled-off; punishing on-ramp.
  Owns the hardcore niche, leaves money and reach on the table.
- Critic no / Buyer yes → sells but doesn't earn its weight; a hook with a
  hollow core, longevity risk once the campaign/launch crowd is through it.
- Buyer no / Critic yes → a great game nobody can pitch; the most fixable
  failure (it's a positioning problem, not a design one).
- All four no on the same axis → that's your headline, lead the verdict with
  it.
