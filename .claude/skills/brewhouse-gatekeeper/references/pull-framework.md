# The Pull Framework

The retention model this skill evaluates against. Read this before reviewing.
A game's *art* gets it to the table once. None of what follows is about art —
it's about what manufactures the craving to set it up again.

## The core claim

Durable replay in a medium-heavy euro is generated by a **signature mechanism
that produces a recurring tension**, decisions that are **coupled or multi-use**
so nothing is throwaway, a **variability engine** for freshness, and a
**legible engine-build arc** that pays off late. Theme earns its keep only
where it is fused to mechanism rather than skinned on top.

But "pull" is not one thing. Different games manufacture different cravings, and
which one you're building is a design decision, not an accident. There are four
distinct engines.

## The four pull engines

**Depth pull — legible loss + a high skill ceiling.**
You replay because you can *see why you lost* and believe you can fix it. Built
from tightly coupled / multi-use decisions, a starved economy, and a system
deep enough that good play visibly beats mediocre play. This is the strongest
and most underused retention engine in euro design, because it costs nothing per
player to maintain — no new content required. Exemplars: Lisboa (regret at
maximum density — every card coupled across 3–4 uses, every misallocation
nameable), Great Western Trail (adaptive mastery against a co-built, moving
target), Orléans (variance you author and therefore take credit for), Agricola
(forced breadth under scarcity; you always finish having neglected something).

**Variety pull — setup variability + combo discovery.**
You replay because the puzzle is *fresh*, not because it's deep. Built from
large card pools, modular setup, drafted/drawn variability. Real and powerful —
but it puts retention on the *content roadmap*. When novelty is the engine,
expansions aren't optional, they're the business model. Exemplars: Wingspan
(hundreds of unique birds, fresh hand every game), Viticulture (the visitor-card
combo lottery — swingy, generous, sometimes near-broken on purpose).

**Comfort pull — frictionless compounding.**
You replay because it's *absorbing and never punishes you*. Low/zero
interaction, an engine tuned to always feel like it's accelerating, sustained
low-grade satisfaction rather than spiky peaks. The pull of being absorbed, not
of being tested. Binds the solo / cozy-heavy audience hard but converts a
narrower slice. Exemplars: Unconscious Mind, the gentle side of Wingspan.

**Fantasy pull — aspirational progression you can feel.**
You replay because optimizing *is* living out a fantasy — the engine-build and
the role-fantasy are the same gesture, so the math carries emotional weight a
pure abstract can't manufacture. Converts fewer people but binds them hardest;
this is how you build a devoted niche rather than a broad hit. Exemplar:
Obsession (reputation gating literally *is* social ascent; you're not building
a machine, you're becoming respectable).

Most games are dominated by one engine and borrow a little from a second.
Naming the dominant one — and whether it's the one the designer *thinks* they
built — is the single most clarifying move in a review.

## The legible-loss test (load-bearing)

For a medium-heavy euro, ask: **can a player finish a game and name the
decision that cost them, and believe a cleaner line was reachable?**

- If **yes** → the game owns depth pull and will outlive its art. Losses are
  diagnosable, so improvement is felt, so players return to climb.
- If **no** → loss is *illegible*. The player can't say why they lost (this is
  Wingspan's structural ceiling — engineered never to sting, which kept the
  comfort audience but caps the depth audience and churns hardcore players
  faster). A medium-heavy game with illegible loss is secretly running on
  variety pull whether or not it intended to — and its retention is therefore
  hostage to the expansion calendar.

Illegible loss in a heavy game is the most dangerous single flaw, because the
weight promises depth the game then fails to deliver: it asks for the
investment of a thinky euro and returns the diagnosability of a light one.

## The designer's fork (apply to the game under review)

Depth and variety want *opposite* tunings:

- **Depth wants** a stable, tight, punishing, low-variance system. Coupled
  decisions, starved economy, few random swings, a high skill ceiling.
- **Variety wants** a loose, generous, modular, high-variance system. Big card
  pools, swingy combos, lots of setup permutations.

You cannot maximize both in the same subsystem. A review's job is partly to
catch a game trying to — e.g. a starved, depth-flavored core bolted to a swingy,
variety-flavored card deck that undermines the legibility the core was building.
Decide which engine each subsystem serves, and flag the ones at war with
themselves.

## How to use this in a review

1. Identify the **signature mechanism** and the **recurring tension** it
   generates. If you can't find one, that's the headline weakness.
2. Check that decisions are **coupled / multi-use**. Throwaway turns are dead
   weight in this weight class.
3. Run the **legible-loss test**. This is the most important single judgment.
4. Name the **dominant pull engine** and whether it matches intent.
5. Flag subsystems whose tuning is **at war** with the intended engine.
